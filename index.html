    About this map

    Scripture, history and tradition

    The New Testament records the deaths of only two apostles: Judas Iscariot and James son of Zebedee. Jewish and Roman historians such as Josephus, Tacitus and Suetonius confirm the world around them. Where the apostles went and how they died comes from early church writers, from Clement of Rome in AD 96 to Eusebius in 325, and from later legends. Each is marked and dated.

    How the verdicts are weighed

    A death counts as “very strong” when several independent witnesses report it within living memory, “probable” when an early tradition is consistent, “uncertain” when early sources disagree, and “unknown” when only late legends survive. For comparison, each case also gives the rating of historian Sean McDowell in The Fate of the Apostles (2015; second edition 2025).

    The dates

    The timeline places the crucifixion and resurrection in AD 30. Many scholars prefer AD 33, which would move the early dates three years later. Dates for Acts and the letters are approximate and still debated.

    Same names, different people

    Philip the evangelist (Acts 6:5; 8:4-40; 21:8), who baptized the Ethiopian official, was one of the Seven, not Philip the apostle. James the Lord’s brother (Galatians 1:19; Acts 15:13), who led the Jerusalem church, is usually seen as a different man from both apostles named James. John Mark is not John the apostle.

    Others called apostles

    The New Testament also calls Barnabas (Acts 14:14), James the Lord’s brother (Galatians 1:19), and Andronicus and Junia (Romans 16:7) apostles, in the wider sense of messengers sent out by the church. This map follows the Twelve, Matthias and Paul.

    The text and the map
